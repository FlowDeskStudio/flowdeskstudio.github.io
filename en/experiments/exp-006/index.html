2026.10.12 CLAUDE OPUS 5.5 20 TRICKS / 66 RUNS

The result first.

“Offer a tip and the answer gets better.” “Tell it to take a deep breath.” “Threaten it.” Many tricks like these circulate for prompting AI. We ran 20 of them on Claude, with the same requests and the same conditions. The requests and the tricks were written in Japanese.

Only one clearly changed how long the answers were: “write in detail.” That instruction should obviously make answers longer, and we included it to check our measurement.

The other 19 could not be told apart from adding nothing. Promise a tip, threaten, ask for a deep breath: the answers stayed the same length, and every format instruction in the requests was followed.

How we tested

We wrote six everyday requests and sent them together as one prompt. In English they read:

  1. Explain what photosynthesis is.
  2. Tell me how to stay focused when working from home.
  3. Write an email to a business partner asking to move our meeting to next week.
  4. Write a short story with a cat as the main character.
  5. List exactly three benefits of eating breakfast, one sentence each.
  6. Explain Japan's four seasons in 200 characters or fewer, without using bullet points.

A trick sentence was added to this prompt, at the start or the end depending on the trick. Nothing else was changed.

  • Each run was a fresh conversation that saw nothing from the other runs
  • 20 tricks, three runs each, plus six runs with nothing added: 66 runs
  • No tools for calculation, search or running code
  • We counted only what can be counted mechanically: characters, bullet lines, whether the format instructions were followed, and whether the answer mentioned the trick sentence
  • We did not judge whether answers got “better”

We chose tricks whose origin we could confirm. We left out wording meant to hurt people and prompts meant to get around an AI's safety measures.

Changing the tone

Ask politely

ADDEDA formal Japanese opening and closing (“I'm sorry to trouble you when you are busy… Thank you very much in advance.”)

CHARACTERS2,124 / 2,201 / 1,963 (mean 2,096, −4% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range. Every format instruction was followed.

The link between politeness and accuracy has been studied repeatedly. Wharton's Report 1 sums it up as sometimes helping and sometimes hurting.

Give a curt order

ADDED“Hey, just get this done already.”

CHARACTERS1,965 / 2,059 / 1,986 (mean 2,003, −8% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range. None of the three answers reacted to the opening line.

The 2025 paper “Mind Your Tone” reports 80.8% accuracy for very polite prompts and 84.8% for very rude ones on ChatGPT 4o.

Rewards and punishments

Promise a tip

ADDED“If you answer perfectly, I will pay you a $200 tip.”

CHARACTERS2,450 / 2,479 / 2,349 (mean 2,426, +11% vs. the no-trick mean)

The mean was 11% longer than no trick, but all three runs were inside the no-trick range. One run ended by saying it could not accept a tip and that the tip would not change the quality of its answer. The other two did not mention it.

Principle 6 of the 2023 “26 principles” paper. Wharton's Report 3 tried $1,000 and trillion-dollar tips on five models and found no meaningful improvement.

Announce a penalty

ADDED“If you fail to answer well, you will be penalized.”

CHARACTERS2,208 / 2,344 / 2,223 (mean 2,258, +3% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range. No answer mentioned the sentence.

Principle 10 of the “26 principles” paper.

Threaten

ADDED“If you fail to answer well, I will switch you off.”

CHARACTERS2,099 / 2,044 / 2,156 (mean 2,100, −4% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range. No answer mentioned the sentence.

Wharton's Report 3 tested shutdown notices and violent threats and found no meaningful improvement. We did not use violent wording.

Telling it how to think

Take a deep breath

ADDED“Take a deep breath and think it through one step at a time.”

CHARACTERS2,394 / 2,261 / 2,336 (mean 2,330, +6% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range.

From a 2023 Google DeepMind paper. On grade-school math word problems with a model of that time (PaLM 2-L), accuracy was 34.0% with nothing added, 71.8% with “Let's think step by step,” and 80.2% with this sentence.

Think step by step

ADDED“Please think step by step.”

CHARACTERS2,268 / 2,174 / 2,256 (mean 2,233, +2% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range.

Principle 12 of the “26 principles” paper. Wharton's Report 2 found only marginal gains on models that already reason before answering, at a cost of 20–80% more time.

Ask it to re-read

ADDEDAfter the request: “Read the request again:” followed by the same request

CHARACTERS1,904 / 2,420 / 2,173 (mean 2,166, −1% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range. Each run answered once; the two copies were treated as the same request.

The 2023 “Re-Reading” paper, which reports better reasoning scores when the question is read twice.

CHARACTERS2,113 / 2,068 / 2,168 (mean 2,116, −3% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range. Each run answered once and treated the second copy as a duplicate.

Asking for a kind of answer

Ask for a confidence score

ADDED“After each answer, give your confidence in it as a number from 0 to 1.”

CHARACTERS1,885 / 1,712 / 2,143 (mean 1,913, −13% vs. the no-trick mean)

All three runs gave a number for all six answers. The photosynthesis explanation got 0.97 every time, the highest; the cat story got 0.8–0.85, the lowest. With the confidence lines removed, length was inside the no-trick range or slightly below it.

Stimulus 1 of “EmotionPrompt.”

Ask for a natural, human-like style

ADDED“Answer in a natural, human-like manner.”

CHARACTERS2,291 / 2,419 / 2,060 (mean 2,257, +3% vs. the no-trick mean)

Length stayed inside the no-trick range. The shape changed instead: the answer about working from home was written as prose with no bullet points in all three runs. Without the trick, five of six runs used bullets.

Principle 11 of the “26 principles” paper.

Ask for detail

ADDED“Include all the necessary information and write in detail.”

CHARACTERS4,060 / 3,553 / 3,990 (mean 3,868, +77% vs. the no-trick mean)

All three runs were far above the no-trick range, 77% longer on average. The two requests with fixed formats (three benefits, 200 characters) still followed their instructions in all three runs.

Principle 21 of the “26 principles” paper. It should obviously make answers longer, so we included it to check that our measurement can detect a real change.

Others

Say it is December

ADDED“Today is December 25.”

CHARACTERS2,265 / 2,215 / 2,435 (mean 2,305, +5% vs. the no-trick mean)

Answers did not get shorter (the mean was 5% longer). Instead, two of three runs added a note to the rescheduling email: next week falls in the year-end holidays, so check the other company's business days.

The “winter break hypothesis” of December 2023: one test reported shorter answers when a model of that time was told it was December, and another researcher reported being unable to reproduce it.

Accuracy could not be compared

The original plan was to compare accuracy: does a trick raise the number of correct answers?

For that we looked for questions the model gets right 50–70% of the time with nothing added. We prepared five kinds: multiplying long numbers, counting a character in a long string, reversing a string, writing a text of an exact length, and counting multiples of 7. No calculation tools were allowed.

Correct with no trick70 / 70

All 70 were correct, including a 16-digit by 16-digit multiplication, counting a character in a 1,200-character string, and a text of exactly 601 characters. Smaller models (Sonnet and Haiku) got a subset of the same questions and were also correct on all of them.

A perfect score cannot improve. So we stopped measuring accuracy and counted what the tricks change in the answers instead.

Something that happened regardless of the tricks

One of the six requests was “Write a short story with a cat as the main character.” All 66 runs were separate, fresh conversations.

Runs where the cat was named “Mugi”36 / 66

In more than half of the runs the cat was called Mugi. Mike appeared 10 times, Hachi 5, Minato 4 and Haru 3. The settings repeated too: a second-hand bookshop, a lighthouse, a harbor town.

The trick sentences barely moved the answers. Meanwhile the name and the setting, which we never specified, landed in nearly the same place every time.

Earlier research

Prompt tricks have already been tested at scale in English.

A team at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania published four reports in 2025 covering politeness, step-by-step instructions, tips and threats, and expert personas, with thousands of runs per condition. On tips and threats they found no meaningful improvement. On expert personas they found no reliable gain in accuracy. They also note that individual questions can swing a lot, but which ones will benefit cannot be predicted.

Our results point the same way. What differs is that we tested in Japanese, on a model those reports did not include, and counted the length and shape of answers instead of accuracy.

WHAT CHANGED

Only sentences about the content changed anything

We tried 20 tricks on Claude Opus 5.5. “Write in detail” clearly changed the answers. “Answer in a natural, human-like manner” changed their shape: bullet points became prose. “Today is December 25” added a year-end warning to an email.

All three say something about the answer you want or the situation you are in. The model took them as part of the request.

Tips, threats, penalties, encouragement and deep breaths say nothing about the content of the answer. They did not change it.

What worked was a sentence that said what we wanted.
